# Website headers and navigation: empirical and standards evidence (education and long-form)

Scope of this pass: public studies and standards on global headers, primary navigation, search, wayfinding, and mobile chrome, read for a public self-paced technical library whose main job is reading long lessons. Accounts are optional. Phone and desktop both matter. Year of the pass: 2026. This file records claims and limits. It does not specify a site design.

Evidence grade is uneven. The only large published task-metric comparison of hidden versus visible website navigation found here is Nielsen Norman Group’s 2016 remote study (news and content sites, not a learning library). Mega-menu, sticky-header, and breadcrumb articles from NN/g are mostly qualitative usability-test syntheses without published success rates. Baymard numbers below are e-commerce benchmarks and moderated tests, not lesson reading. WCAG 2.2 text was taken from the 12 December 2024 Recommendation. No large-sample study of a learning-site header, a “Continue / Resume” control, or a command palette versus a visible search field was found.

# How many primary navigation items do people handle well, and what happens with mega menus?

### Takeaway

There is no supported fixed cap such as 7 items. People do not have to memorize a visible menu, so Miller’s 7±2 does not set the count; label specificity, scope, and whether choices are visible at once matter more. Mega menus can beat long scrolling dropdowns in qualitative testing when groups are scannable and everything is visible without scrolling, and they fail when grouping, hover timing, touch size, or magnification hide choices. On phones, NN/g’s quantitative study led them to show 4 or fewer top-level links and hide the rest.

### Cited Findings

- “A common misconception is that you should have no more than 7 items in your navigation’s top tier. This is a misunderstanding of” George Miller’s 1956 working-memory paper. “Users aren’t forced to memorize the items in your menus. They are available right there in front of them to read.” The count “depends” on breadth of content, and “a menu with 10 or more top-level items that are clearly described can be much more usable” than three vague labels such as Products, Services, and About. Items at the beginning and end are easier to spot than items in the middle. Source is a 24 May 2019 NN/g video by Page Laubheimer; the transcript excerpt does not include a participant count or a success-rate table. — [NN/g, How Many Items in a Navigation Menu?](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/number-items-navigation-menu/)
- “Is it okay to have more than 7–9 top-tier categories in the global navigation? (Spoiler alert: it is okay, you just need to plan appropriately for it.)” Forcing a broad set into a horizontal bar leads teams to shrink type, crowd items, or shorten labels. In one consulting-site example, that “reduced” findability and raised interaction cost because people opened several generic top-level categories. A vertical left navigation “easily accommodated 13 global navigation categories” and let labels stay specific. No task-time or success numbers are given. Article date was not on the excerpt retrieved. — [NN/g, Left-Side Vertical Navigation on Desktop](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/vertical-nav/)
- “Generally speaking, it’s a good idea to consider alphabetical ordering for lists with 20 or more items. If a list has fewer than 10 categories, there is probably not much benefit to alphabetizing them (unless they are unique terms that people will be specifically seeking).” Published 4 January 2015. This is an IA guideline from observed practice, not a timed menu experiment. — [NN/g, Top 3 IA Questions about Navigation Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ia-questions-navigation-menus/)
- Hick’s Law, as stated by NN/g on 6 July 2018: “the more choices you present to your users, the longer it takes them to reach a decision. However, combining Hick’s Law with other design techniques can make long menus easy to use.” No decision-time constant was extracted from the video page. — [NN/g, Hick’s Law: Designing Long Menu Lists](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/hicks-law/)
- Eyetracking claim, 28 April 2008: “users tend to rapidly move their eyes down the left-hand side of lists. People read the rest of a list item only if something catches their eyes in these left-most one or two words.” Right-aligned menus “severely reduce the speed” of scanning. “USING ALL CAPS … reduces legibility by about 10%.” The 10% figure is stated without the study citation on the article page retrieved. — [NN/g, Right-Justified Navigation Menus Impede Scannability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/right-justified-navigation-menus/)
- Mega menus, Jakob Nielsen and Angie Li, 26 March 2017: “We know from user testing that mega menus work.” Arguments given, not a results table: regular dropdowns “hide most of the user’s options”; scrolling “hides the options at the top,” so people must use short-term memory; mega menus “show everything at a glance.” They also support grouping and images. Characteristics they treat as definitional: two-dimensional grouped panels, “Everything visible at once — no scrolling.” No N, task-success rate, or time delta is published in the article. — [NN/g, Mega Menus Work Well for Site Navigation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mega-menus-work-well/)
- Same 2017 article, hover timing from testing: do not open too fast. “The mouse should remain stationary for 0.5 seconds” before showing a hover menu; then display “within 0.1 seconds”; keep it until the pointer has been outside both the item and the panel “for 0.5 seconds”; then remove it “in less than 0.1 seconds.” Exception: if the pointer is moving from the nav item into the panel, keep the panel open (the “diagonal problem”). “Hiding the search box within a mega menu” is called bad because “the search box should be continuously visible.” For low vision and screen magnification, “only a small portion of the mega menu might be visible,” and users “may assume that the visible content is all the content.” “Tiny options inside mega menus cause selection errors on touchscreens.” — [NN/g, Mega Menus Work Well](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mega-menus-work-well/)
- Earlier qualitative report, Jakob Nielsen, 15 November 2010: studies had found “good results from sites using mega menus,” but deployed menus failed when divisions were not meaningful and the organizing principle was not obvious “at a quick glance, which is all that web users are likely to grant.” — [NN/g, Mega Menus Gone Wrong](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mega-menus-gone-wrong/)
- Menu checklist, 7 June 2024 (PDF dated 6 June 2024). Expected locations: primary navigation in the website header; utility navigation “above the primary navigation and search, towards the top”; local navigation “along the left-hand side on desktop”; footer at the bottom. “On large screens, don’t cover the entire screen when megamenus (or submenus) are open.” “A simple dropdown menu works well for one-tier navigation but becomes frustrating with two tiers. It is highly inadvisable for more than two tiers.” If there are multiple submenu levels, “use either a mega menu (that can support 2–3 tiers of information reasonably well), or send users to routing pages.” Checklist also says: “Use click-activated rather than hover-activated submenus to improve accessibility across devices.” That click preference is a later guideline than the 2017 hover-timing recipe; both are published by NN/g. No new experiment is attached to the 2024 checklist. — [NN/g, Menu-Design Checklist](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/menu-design/) and [checklist PDF](https://media.nngroup.com/media/articles/attachments/PDF_Menu-Design-Checklist.pdf)
- Mobile subcategory guidance, 16 July 2017: “Submenus work well when they contain just a few subcategories (typically less than 6).” Decision rule stated: fewer than 6 subcategories, a submenu or accordion may be appropriate; between 6 and 15, consider a section menu; beyond that, a category landing page. These thresholds are design rules from testing, not a dose-response curve. — [NN/g, Mobile Subnavigation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-subnavigation/)
- Phone top-level count from the 2016 hidden-navigation study (methods under the mobile question): “If your site has 4 or fewer top-level navigation links, display them as visible links. If your site has more than 4 … the only reasonable solution is to hide some of these.” The “4” is their recommendation after measuring the cost of hiding, not a finding that 5 links cause a measured failure. — [NN/g, Hamburger Menus and Hidden Navigation Hurt UX Metrics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)
- Baymard, main-navigation example catalog (page undated in the scrape; catalog size was 1,816 annotated screenshots from a benchmark of 344 e-commerce sites in one capture, and 1,569 from 325 in another): “Hover-based drop-down menus (aka ‘mega menus’) are currently used as the main navigation at 88% of all top US e-commerce sites — yet our testing shows that drop-down menus require the utmost attention to interaction details if they are to perform well.” The page does not give participant N or the task-failure rate behind that sentence. Separate testing note on the same page: minor visual details “severely” misled subjects about hierarchy, scope, and how to move back up. — [Baymard, Main Navigation examples](https://baymard.com/ecommerce-design-examples/27-drop-down-menu)
- Baymard, Edward Scott, 16 November 2022: on mobile, with no hover, users still need the broad parent category, not only the deepest link. “Providing a ‘View All’ option in the mobile main navigation” was the pattern that met that need, “yet only 24% of sites implement this well” (also “only 24% of sites get completely right” in their e-commerce benchmark). That 24% is sites meeting a guideline, not a user success rate. — [Baymard, View All in mobile main navigation](https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-main-nav-view-all)

### Inferences

- For a library whose top level is a handful of stable destinations (lessons/tracks, roadmaps, practice, interview prep, search), the 2016 “4 or fewer, show them” phone rule and the 2015 “tab bars get hard above about 5” pattern are the closest numeric guidance. They are not a measured optimum for reading tasks.
- A mega menu is evidenced as a fix for a wide catalog with a second tier people must compare, which is the e-commerce and large-IA case. The same sources say a mega menu that scrolls, cascades, hides search, or depends on a fast hover is where the pattern fails. A track-and-lesson library can be deep without being a product catalog; nothing retrieved measures mega menus on that job.
- The 2017 hover recipe and the 2024 “use click, not hover” line are in tension. WCAG 1.4.13 (next section) allows hover only if the panel is dismissible, hoverable, and persistent. That standard, not the 0.5-second recipe, is the testable requirement.

### Gaps

- No peer-reviewed or NN/g table maps number of header items to task success or time on a documentation or course site.
- Mega-menu “they work” is not backed, in the free articles, by N, confidence intervals, or a dropdown control condition with numbers.
- Hick’s Law video was not transcribed for a time-per-item figure. Miller 1956 was not re-read; the claim used here is NN/g’s rejection of applying it to menus.
- Baymard’s 88% and the qualitative “misleading details” finding are e-commerce. The premium reports that would contain session counts were not opened.

# What does research say about sticky headers, hide-on-scroll headers, and header height?

### Takeaway

NN/g’s 2021 sticky-header article is expert synthesis, not a timed experiment: a sticky header can make navigation, search, and utilities available without scrolling to the top, and it fails when it is tall, low-contrast, or animated in a way that covers or distracts from content. Hide-on-scroll (show again when the user scrolls up) is described as a mobile compromise that “works moderately well,” with a known false positive when the user scrolls up only to reread. No universal pixel height was found. The concrete guidance is minimum tap size and text size, then as little extra vertical chrome as possible; one phone example they call reasonable is a 13:1 content-to-chrome ratio, and one they call poor is 2:1.

### Cited Findings

- Page Laubheimer, 4 April 2021. Definition: sticky headers stay put while the user scrolls. A partially sticky header “reappears at the top of the page as soon as the user starts scrolling up.” “When done appropriately, sticky headers allow users to quickly access the navigation, search, and utility-navigation elements without scrolling up to the top of the page. They increase the discoverability of the elements in the header and the chance that users will take advantage of them.” “When implemented poorly, sticky headers are annoying, distracting, and obstruct page content.” No participant count, no before/after task time. — [NN/g, Sticky Headers: 5 Ways to Make Them Better](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)
- Height: sticky headers “take up space on the screen that could be used for content,” “even more important on mobile.” Keep the smallest height that still has readable text and tappable targets. On touchscreens, “tap targets are a minimum of 1cm × 1cm,” text “approximately 16pt” (adjust for the typeface), then “minimize any additional vertical height.” On desktop, do not spend height on an oversized logo. Worked examples on an iPhone 11 Pro, browser chrome excluded: The New Yorker’s sticky header “has a 13:1 content-to-chrome ratio … a reasonable space tradeoff.” Lollar Pickups “has a miserly 2:1,” with a large translucent logo and a second sticky tab bar. Translucent headers are rejected: low contrast “make[s] the semivisible content hard to read and frequently annoy and distract users.” The header “must be an opaque color, different from the background.” — [NN/g, Sticky Headers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)
- Motion: “it’s best to not use animation at all and simply keep the header in place.” If a large header must shrink, “the header should shrink at roughly typical scrolling speed and should not disappear, jump, or otherwise startle the user.” Do not use a delayed “stalker menu” that catches up after the scroll. — [NN/g, Sticky Headers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)
- Hide-on-scroll: “Partially persistent headers are most frequently seen on mobile sites and, in some cases, offer the best of both worlds: a header that is easily accessible from anywhere on the page, but stays unobtrusive while you read and scroll down.” “This style works moderately well.” It “assume[s] that any scroll up is an indication that the user wants to access the header.” That is false when “the user may simply want to reread a previous paragraph,” and then “the animations … can be distracting.” They recommend a slide-in of “roughly 300–400ms,” triggered by scrolling up “a few pixels,” and not so sensitive that “an unsteady hand” opens it. The 300–400ms figure points at their animation-duration article; this page does not show a new timing study. — [NN/g, Sticky Headers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)
- Whether to use one at all: “by using a sticky header you give away some screen real estate on every single page.” Ask whether login, main categories, or utilities are “likely to be needed often or at any point during a session,” or whether people “stay in a single category for their entire time.” “The answers … will vary depending on the type of site and the tasks and content that it supports.” — [NN/g, Sticky Headers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)
- Older observation, Jakob Nielsen, 8 January 2000, still the framing NN/g uses for chrome versus reading: on a new page, “users look straight at the content and ignore the navigation areas.” Studies “typically find” that if content is irrelevant, other design does not matter, and on arrival people “ignore navigation bars and other global design elements.” No sample size on that page. — [NN/g, Is Navigation Useful?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/is-navigation-useful/)
- Standards consequence of a sticky bar, not a usability-performance result: WCAG 2.2 lists sticky headers as a typical way to hide keyboard focus. Failure F110 is “a sticky footer or header completely hiding focused elements.” A documented sufficient technique is CSS `scroll-padding` so the focused item scrolls clear of the bar. Partial coverage still passes Level AA 2.4.11 and fails Level AAA 2.4.12. Normative date used here: Recommendation 12 December 2024. — [Understanding 2.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-not-obscured-minimum.html) and [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#focus-not-obscured-minimum)

### Inferences

- The reading job pulls against a tall persistent header: the 2000 finding is that people attend to content first, and the 2021 article’s own test for keeping a sticky header is whether header controls are needed repeatedly in one session. A long lesson is the case they flag, where the user may stay in one section. That is an application of their cost-benefit question, not a measured lesson-reading result.
- Hide-on-scroll is specifically motivated as a way to give reading space back. The same article says the trigger (any upward scroll) is wrong for rereading, which is a normal behavior in technical lessons. No study retrieved measured comprehension or reread errors with that pattern.
- “13:1” and “2:1” are single-site illustrations on one phone, not norms. The 1cm target is NN/g’s touch guideline and is stricter than WCAG 2.2 AA (24 CSS pixels) and aligned in spirit with AAA (44 CSS pixels); they are different standards (see the WCAG section).

### Gaps

- No A/B or task-time study of sticky versus static versus hide-on-scroll headers was found, including none on reading speed, scroll depth, or lesson completion.
- No researched maximum header height in pixels for desktop or for a given viewport.
- The 300–400ms animation figure was not traced to its underlying study in this pass.
- Baymard was not found, in open articles retrieved here, to publish a sticky-header performance number.

# Where should search live, and when is a command palette better or worse than a visible field?

### Takeaway

Repeated NN/g findings say people look first at the upper right for a search box, then across the top, and that an open field beats a link or an icon-only control on desktop. Hiding search inside a menu adds steps and, in the 2016 navigation study, is what people fall back to when navigation is weak. A command palette (a hidden keyboard launcher) had no comparative study against a visible field for a public content site. The closest 2026 experiment found is about mobile command selection, and it favored a visible one-layer toolbar over keyboard methods. A palette is described in practitioner pattern guides, not in user tests, as faster for experts who already know the shortcut and weaker for discovery.

### Cited Findings

- Jakob Nielsen, 12 May 2001: users “scan the homepage looking for ‘the little box where I can type.’” “On homepages, search should be a type-in field and not a link.” “Put the search box at the top of the page, usually in the right hand corner (though the left works almost as well).” The field should be “wide enough to contain the typical query.” When useit.com replaced a search link with a box, “search engine use increased by 91%.” That is one site’s before/after, not a controlled multi-site experiment. “Interior pages may use a search link if they have a very simple design; complex interior pages should use a search box.” — [NN/g, Search: Visible and Simple](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/search-visible-and-simple/)
- NN/g magnifying-glass article (date not captured in this pass): “Provide an easily identifiable search box in the upper right-hand corner of the page, with an open text-field accompanied by a Search button.” “People first look to the upper-right corner for search. If they don’t find it there, they start scanning the top of the page. Among sites that had the search icon on the left, those that used a large, empty open-text field fared best.” Desktop: “retain an open text-entry field.” Tablets: “best to retain the text field.” “When a site is viewed on small screens … the text box can be hidden until the user touches the magnifying-glass icon.” — [NN/g, The Magnifying-Glass Icon in Search Design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/magnifying-glass-icon/)
- NN/g intranet-search article, 22 May 2022, from studies of intranets rather than public course sites: “Use a search box, not just a search icon. Place it in the upper right corner of the global navigation menu.” An icon “made the search less visible and forced users to click on the magnifying glass icon to expand the search box. Users had to then click again to set their cursor’s focus.” An exposed bar “speeds up the task of performing a search.” Multiple search boxes “caused confusion.” Participant count is in the paid or longer study writeup; it is not in the excerpt. — [NN/g, Intranet-Search Essentials](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/intranet-search/)
- E-commerce search writeup (date not captured): search “is almost uniformly located alongside the global navigation,” which removed older failures (missing boxes, menus covering the field). “Users look for a wide, empty field or a magnifying glass icon.” Icon-only “works fine” on mobile; “on desktop, sites should still use a simple, visible empty box.” Named ongoing failure: “Low-visibility search boxes (for example, behind a tiny magnifying glass icon on a large desktop screen, or hidden under a hamburger menu on mobile).” — [NN/g, The State of Ecommerce Search](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/state-ecommerce-search/)
- Mega-menu article, 26 March 2017: “hiding the search box within a mega menu” fails because search “should be continuously visible on the page rather than displayed only when users activate the mega menu.” — [NN/g, Mega Menus Work Well](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mega-menus-work-well/)
- Navigation does not replace search, and search does not replace navigation. Raluca Budiu, 15 November 2015, summarizing prior NN/g work: users often “don’t know what to search for”; “coming up with a good query and typing it requires more mental effort” than recognizing a link; “users are notoriously bad at formulating good queries”; “site search often works a lot more poorly than” web search engines. On mobile, both search and navigation “occupy screen space.” — [NN/g, Basic Patterns for Mobile Navigation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-navigation-patterns/)
- In the 2016 hidden-navigation study, content “discoverability” was defined as finding the target with navigation rather than search when it was not linked from the homepage. Hidden navigation lowered that measure by “more than 20%” versus visible or combo navigation. Desktop users were “more likely to fail” at that navigational discovery than mobile users, “explained by desktop users’ increased propensity to use search.” So a visible field is what people use when the menu has low information scent; it is not evidence that search should be the only way around. — [NN/g, Hamburger Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)
- Apparel is a counter-example on when search is secondary. Baymard, 23 July 2024: in apparel and accessories testing, “100% of test participants primarily relied on the main navigation,” and “search is used as a fallback … by only a small subgroup of users (10% in testing), when the main navigation or browsing fails.” They tell apparel sites to invest in categories over search. This is a domain result, not a documentation result. The session count is not in the open article. — [Baymard, Category navigation versus search](https://baymard.com/research-articles/apparel-search)
- Command-palette versus visible controls, not a website study: a Graphics Interface 2026 paper PDF (N=120) compared mobile command selection: chording, hotkeys, a one-layer toolbar with all commands visible, and a toolbar that required navigation. “The toolbar with visible commands was substantially faster than the keyboard-based techniques.” In the training game, selection was “between 347ms and 459ms faster” than the other mechanisms “(27%–36%).” The toolbar that required an extra navigation step “was slower than all other methods,” and in that case “shortcut mechanisms can be a viable alternative.” The PDF’s ACM ISBN is an unfilled template (`978-1-4503-XXXX-X/18/06`), so treat bibliographic details as unverified. The tasks were mobile typing and command selection, not finding a lesson on a public site. — [GI ’26 PDF, University of Waterloo host](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~dvogel/gi2026/papers/1026b.pdf)
- WCAG 2.1.4 Character Key Shortcuts (Level A), which matters if a palette is opened by a single character: if a shortcut “is implemented in content using only letter … punctuation, number, or symbol characters,” the user must be able to turn it off, remap it to include a non-printable key such as Ctrl or Alt, or the shortcut must work “only when that component has focus.” A shortcut that already includes Ctrl or Command is outside the “using only letter…” condition. Recommendation 12 December 2024. — [WCAG 2.2, 2.1.4](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#character-key-shortcuts)
- Practitioner pattern pages (not studies) say to use a visible search field when the job is “finding existing content” and discovery matters, and a command palette when the job is “actions, destinations, and recent items” for keyboard-first users. They rate palette discoverability “weak to medium” and visible search “strong” for novices. No sample. — [UX Patterns, Search Field vs Command Palette](https://uxpatterns.dev/pattern-guide/search-field-vs-command-palette)

### Inferences

- For a public library where many visitors are not logged in and are not keyboard experts, the sourced claim is that search has to be seen. The desktop evidence points to an open field at the top, conventionally the upper right. The mobile evidence allows collapsing that field behind an icon because the screen is small, with the explicit cost of extra taps.
- A command palette would add a hidden expert path. Nothing retrieved shows it outperforming a visible field for first-time or occasional readers. The 2026 mobile study points the other way when all commands fit on one visible layer, and points toward shortcuts only after a visible control already requires extra navigation. A single-key opener would also have to meet 2.1.4; a modified shortcut such as Ctrl+K or Command+K would not, by the wording of that criterion.
- Search and a small visible nav are complements in the NN/g account. Apparel’s 10% search rate is a warning not to treat “people prefer browsing” as universal: that sample was choosing among products they could recognize, on sites whose categories were usable.

### Gaps

- No NN/g, Baymard, or peer-reviewed comparison of command palettes, “slash to search,” or Command-K against a persistent field on documentation, course, or marketing sites. Adoption articles about VS Code, Linear, or Superhuman were not treated as evidence.
- The 91% search-use increase is one homepage in 2001.
- Query-length and success-rate numbers for technical-education search were not found. The magnifying-glass article’s test size was not opened.
- Whether icon-only search on phones still costs the same discoverability in 2026 as in the older NN/g tests was not re-measured in sources found here.

# What does research say about Continue / Resume, progress indicators, and breadcrumbs for multi-page learning content?

### Takeaway

No study found here tests a “Continue” or “Resume” control in a learning header, or a lesson-completion meter, against task success or return rate. What is sourced: breadcrumbs help orientation and one-step moves up a hierarchy, especially after a deep link, and they are a poor fit for flat or purely linear sites and for tiny wrapped mobile trails. E-commerce research wants a history-style “back to results” as well as a hierarchy trail, which conflicts with NN/g’s hierarchy-only rule. Progress-indicator research that has numbers is about waiting for a system, not about course progress: a moving indicator made people willing to wait about three times longer than no indicator in one summarized experiment. “You are here” is a WCAG AAA requirement (2.4.8), not AA.

### Cited Findings

- Breadcrumbs, Page Laubheimer, 23 December 2018, last reviewed 1 September 2026. NN/g “has been recommending breadcrumbs since 1995.” They support wayfinding: current location in the hierarchy, and one-click access to ancestors. They matter “when [users] skip some of these levels (for example, because they arrived … from an external link).” They “should not replace the global navigation” or local navigation. They “should display the current location in the site’s hierarchical structure, not the session history.” History trails “become long and confusing” and do not help people who “arrive on a deep page directly.” For polyhierarchy, show one canonical path, not several, and do not personalize the trail per visit. The current page is the last item and is not a link. Do not insert category labels that have no page. “Breadcrumbs aren’t necessary (or useful) for sites with flat hierarchies that are only 1 or 2 levels deep, or sites that are linear in structure.” A linear feature (NPR’s history-of-color minisite) is the example given for skipping breadcrumbs. No success rate or time data in this article. — [NN/g, Breadcrumbs: 11 Design Guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/breadcrumbs/)
- Same article, mobile: “the cost of using breadcrumbs can quickly overwhelm the benefits.” Do not wrap to multiple lines (an REI example pushed the product name below the fold on an iPhone X). Do not shrink links below “at least 1 cm × 1 cm.” “Consider shortening the breadcrumb trail to include only the last level(s)” on mobile; Best Buy’s mobile site showed only the parent, while desktop showed the full trail. “This advice conflicts with” always linking Home, “and should only be done on mobile.” — [NN/g, Breadcrumbs](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/breadcrumbs/)
- Jakob Nielsen, 10 April 2007: “User testing shows many benefits and no downsides to breadcrumbs for secondary navigation.” People “might overlook this small design element, but they never misinterpret breadcrumb trails or have trouble operating them.” He did not have “an exact number for the current percentage of breadcrumb users” because breadcrumbs were “not important enough for a dedicated study.” “Every year we see more people use breadcrumbs.” “80% of this year’s award-winning intranets use breadcrumbs” (adoption among winners, not a user-success metric). Hierarchy, not the Hansel-and-Gretel history model. — [NN/g, Breadcrumb Navigation Increasingly Useful](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/breadcrumb-navigation-useful/)
- Conflict on history versus hierarchy. Baymard, Jamie Holst, 10 December 2013, from a homepage-and-category usability study, benchmark of 50 major e-commerce sites: “68% suffer from sub-par breadcrumb implementations: 45% have only one type of breadcrumb (typically hierarchy-based, lacking the history-based), and 23% don’t have breadcrumbs at all.” Without a trail, subjects made “drastic scope jumps” or got stuck on the product. The common use was “to return to the category the test subject just came from.” A hierarchy link drops filters and sort; a history link (“Back to results”) keeps them but does not help someone who landed from outside. They recommend both. July 2014 note on the same page: if “both at the same time” is required, “the number goes up to 94%”; a May 2014 search benchmark put the share lacking both types at 92%. Session count is not in the article. This is product-list state (filters), not lesson position. — [Baymard, Two types of breadcrumbs](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-breadcrumbs)
- Baymard on “where am I,” Christian Holst-era article, 19 May 2020 (full text not opened; claim is from the public summary): when the current scope is not highlighted in the main navigation, it is “unnecessarily difficult to quickly determine where they are,” harder to learn the structure, and harder to move to another top-level scope. “66% of sites don’t highlight the user’s current scope,” also stated as “66% of desktop sites.” A 2025 Baymard LinkedIn post about a later mobile homepage-and-category benchmark says “95% of sites fail at this,” with “16,000+ performance scores” and “67% of mobile sites” mediocre or worse. The 66% and 95% figures are different years and, on the wording retrieved, different device scopes; they are benchmark compliance, not user error rates. The 2025 article body was not opened. — [Baymard, Highlight current scope](https://baymard.com/research-articles/highlight-users-navigation-scope) and [Baymard LinkedIn, 8 October 2025](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/baymard-institute_homepage-navigation-ux-best-practices-2025-activity-7381659646127919106-qtlg)
- Baymard, Edward Scott, 3 August 2021: “58% of sites provide links on the homepage to scoped product lists that result in users becoming disoriented.” “Mobile users are simply more at risk of becoming disoriented compared to desktop users” and “need more support” for place in the hierarchy, “e.g., through well-implemented breadcrumbs.” — [Baymard, Full scope for links on mobile homepages](https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-homepage-provide-full-scope)
- Peer-reviewed, not a course site. Gerontechnology abstract (page dated 15 July 2015 in the search record; PDF not read past the abstract): two experiments on age and web navigation. Experiment 1, N=44, compared basic aids with breadcrumbs plus a dynamic side-tree. “Search was more efficient for both age groups in the BC-DST condition.” Age differences in search time and recall of structure “could be eliminated by controlling for working memory and processing speed.” Experiment 2, N=61, dynamic side-tree only: “Navigation tools did not improve performance in either age group.” Age was “not associated with the number of pages or repeat pages visited.” The abstract does not separate the breadcrumb effect from the side-tree in Experiment 1. — [Gerontechnology PDF](https://journal.gerontechnology.org/archives/736-738-1-PB.pdf)
- WCAG 2.4.8 Location (Level AAA), not required at AA: “Information about the user’s location within a set of web pages is available.” Recommendation 12 December 2024. — [WCAG 2.2, 2.4.8](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#location)
- W3C cognitive-accessibility supplemental pattern, not a success criterion and not a user study (page crawled 2 October 2026; pattern text is guidance): “Provide breadcrumbs, a ‘how I got here’ button, or heading to help the user orientate themselves.” In a multi-step process, show “the steps completed, the current step, the steps pending, and any important choices.” Stated benefit: someone who loses focus can continue “without reading a great deal of content or restarting,” and seeing they are “half-way through” helps them judge whether they can finish. The pattern explicitly separates orientation (breadcrumbs / how I got here) from step progress. — [WAI, Make Each Step Clear](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o1p04-clear-steps/)
- Wait-state progress, not course progress. Katie Sherwin, NN/g, 26 October 2014: “Users experience higher satisfaction with a site and are willing to wait longer when the site uses a dynamic progress indicator.” Citing Nah (2004), Behaviour & Information Technology, Vol. 23, No. 3, University of Nebraska-Lincoln: people who saw a continuous progress bar “experienced higher satisfaction and were willing to wait on average 3 times longer” than a group with no indicator. The original Nah paper was not opened. NN/g’s own rules on that page: feedback immediately; looped spinner for about 2–10 seconds; percent-done for about 10 seconds or more; do not use a static “Loading…” and do not rely on “don’t click again” text. “Instead of showing a percentage number, consider showing the number of steps.” Separate claim, study not named in the article: “for surveys, progress indicators that start fast and end slow reduce drop-off rates,” whereas wait animations should start slower and finish faster so the end does not stall. — [NN/g, Progress Indicators](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)
- Jakob Nielsen, 8 August 2026, on his own site, writing about system waits and agent steps, not LMS completion: “Show a progress indicator when response time exceeds 10 seconds, preferably as a percent-done bar.” “‘Step 2 of 4’ is a progress indicator too, working for the same reason: bounded waits are bearable.” This restates his long-standing response-time limits; it is not a new experiment. — [UX Tigers, Progress Indicators Ease the Wait](https://www.uxtigers.com/post/progress-indicators)
- NN/g status-tracker video, Megan Brown, 11 May 2026, is about delivery and service-process tracking, not lessons. The relevant sourced line is discoverability: “if users don’t know the status tracker exists, they won’t use it.” One method given is to show it immediately after the user completes a process. — [NN/g, Status Trackers](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/status-trackers/) and [article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/status-tracker-progress-update/)
- Visibility of system status, Aurora Harley, 3 June 2018: feedback, “or a progress indicator when a process needs a little longer,” tells people the system registered the action so they do not repeat it. A mobile eyetracking clip showed a participant looking between a button and the top of the screen “where a progress indicator for the page load typically appears.” Qualitative. — [NN/g, Visibility of System Status](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visibility-system-status/)

### Inferences

- For multi-page lessons, the sourced wayfinding tools split by job. Hierarchy breadcrumbs are evidenced for “where is this page in the track?” after search or a shared link. They are explicitly not for “what did I click last?” NN/g’s linear-site exception matters: a lesson sequence that is only previous/next is the case where they say a breadcrumb adds little, and local previous/next or a short step label is the structure they point at instead.
- Baymard’s history breadcrumb exists to restore filter and sort state. A learning analogue would be returning to a search or a practice-filter result, not replaying the browser stack. That analogy is not tested in the Baymard study.
- A “Continue / Resume” control and a lesson progress meter are different claims. The wait-indicator literature supports telling people that a system is working and how much waiting is left. The COGA pattern and the step-count line support showing completed, current, and remaining steps in a multi-step process. Neither is evidence that a header “Resume” button raises return rate or completion on an optional-account course site. The status-tracker finding that does transfer is negative: if people are not told the control exists, they do not use it.
- The Gerontechnology result is the only controlled breadcrumb-related efficiency result found, and it is confounded with a side tree, small, and not a course.

### Gaps

- No retrieved study of “Continue,” “Resume,” “pick up where you left off,” reading-position memory, or course-progress bars on MOOCs, docs, or coding-lesson sites, with completion, time-to-resume, or error outcomes.
- NN/g breadcrumb articles do not quantify how often people use the trail or how much time it saves. The 2007 “no downsides” claim is qualitative and old; the 2018/2026 article does not add numbers.
- Experiment 1 in the aging study does not isolate breadcrumbs. Full methods were not read.
- Survey progress-bar drop-off research is alluded to by NN/g without a citation on the page that was fetched.

# Which WCAG 2.2 requirements apply directly to a site header?

### Takeaway

The header is repeated content, a set of controls, and often a sticky layer, so the criteria that bind it are skip/landmarks (2.4.1), keyboard access and no trap (2.1.1, 2.1.2), focus order (2.4.3), name/role/value and label-in-name (4.1.2, 2.5.3), visible focus (2.4.7), focus not fully covered by the sticky bar (2.4.11 AA; fully uncovered is 2.4.12 AAA), target size (2.5.8 AA at 24 CSS pixels, 2.5.5 AAA at 44), and hover/focus menus that can be dismissed, hovered, and kept up (1.4.13). Escape is the example dismiss key in the understanding documents, not a success criterion that says “menus must close on Escape.” WCAG 2.2 used here is the W3C Recommendation of 12 December 2024. 4.1.1 Parsing was removed; 4.1.2 was not.

### Cited Findings

- Document status: “W3C Recommendation 12 December 2024.” New in 2.2 and relevant to headers: 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum) AA, 2.4.12 Focus Not Obscured (Enhanced) AAA, 2.4.13 Focus Appearance AAA, 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) AA, 3.2.6 Consistent Help A. “WCAG 2.2 has removed one success criterion, 4.1.1 Parsing.” — [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)
- Skip link, 2.4.1 Bypass Blocks (Level A): “A mechanism is available to bypass blocks of content that are repeated on multiple web pages.” Intent names “navigation links, header content, and advertising frames.” Sufficient techniques include “Adding a link at the top of each page that goes directly to the main content area” (G1), ARIA landmarks (ARIA11), and headings at the start of sections (H69). Benefits named: screen-reader users avoid hearing “all header content and dozens of navigation links on every page”; keyboard users otherwise “might have to make dozens of keystrokes” before the main content, which “may cause severe physical pain”; magnifier users otherwise re-scan the header. Example: without a skip link, “a keyboard user needs to tab through approximately 40 links” and “the screen reader user has to listen to 200 words.” Note 3 of 1.4.13: a skip link that appears on keyboard focus “do[es] not present additional content” and is not covered by the hover/focus popup rules. — [Understanding 2.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/bypass-blocks.html)
- 2.1.1 Keyboard (Level A): “All functionality of the content is operable through a keyboard interface without requiring specific timings for individual keystrokes,” except where the function itself depends on the path of the pointer. Hover-only menus fail this if there is no keyboard way to open and use them. Understanding 1.4.13 says content that appears on pointer hover “should also be able to be triggered by keyboard focus” via 2.1.1. — [WCAG 2.2, 2.1.1](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#keyboard) and [Understanding 1.4.13](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/content-on-hover-or-focus.html)
- 2.1.2 No Keyboard Trap (Level A): “If keyboard focus can be moved to a component of the page using a keyboard interface, then focus can be moved away from that component using only a keyboard interface, and, if it requires more than unmodified arrow or tab keys or other standard exit methods, the user is advised of the method for moving focus away.” It applies to the whole page even if the menu is not otherwise used for conformance. The criterion does not name the Escape key. — [WCAG 2.2, 2.1.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#no-keyboard-trap)
- 2.4.3 Focus Order (Level A): if the page is navigated sequentially and order affects meaning or operation, “focusable components receive focus in an order that preserves meaning and operability.” — [WCAG 2.2, 2.4.3](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#focus-order)
- 2.4.7 Focus Visible (Level AA): “Any keyboard operable user interface has a mode of operation where the keyboard focus indicator is visible.” — [WCAG 2.2, 2.4.7](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#focus-visible)
- 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum) (Level AA): “When a user interface component receives keyboard focus, the component is not entirely hidden due to author-created content.” “Typical types of content that can overlap focused items are sticky footers, sticky headers, and non-modal dialogs.” Partial hiding passes AA. “For best visibility, none of the component receiving focus should be obscured,” which is 2.4.12 AAA. The check is the component, not an external focus ring; a fully hidden ring can still fail 2.4.7. Note 2: user-opened content may cover the focused item if the user can reveal it “without advancing the keyboard focus,” and the understanding document lists “using the Escape key to dismiss the obscuring content” as one such method. A sticky header that entirely covers the next focused link fails; documented fixes are `scroll-padding` (technique C43) and failure F110. Cookie banners and chat layers are in the same failure class. — [Understanding 2.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-not-obscured-minimum.html) and [normative 2.4.11](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#focus-not-obscured-minimum)
- 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) (Level AA): pointer targets are “at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels,” or undersized targets are spaced so that a 24 CSS-pixel circle centered on each bounding box does not intersect another target or another such circle. Exceptions include inline links in a sentence, an equivalent larger control, unmodified user-agent controls, and essential presentations (maps). The requirement “does not change” with browser zoom. The understanding document’s menu illustration: items “24 CSS pixels” tall pass; items “only 18 CSS pixels in height” fail when the spacing circles hit the next item. “As a best practice” the document points authors who can do so to 2.5.5. — [Understanding 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)
- 2.5.5 Target Size (Enhanced) (Level AAA): “at least 44 by 44 CSS pixels,” with equivalent, inline, user-agent, and essential exceptions. — [WCAG 2.2, 2.5.5](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#target-size-enhanced)
- 1.4.13 Content on Hover or Focus (Level AA), the criterion that actually governs header menus and tooltips: if hover or focus shows extra content and then hides it, that content must be dismissible “without moving pointer hover or keyboard focus,” hoverable (the pointer can move onto the extra content “without the additional content disappearing”), and persistent until the trigger is removed, the user dismisses it, or the information is invalid. Understanding text: provide dismissal “such as pressing the Esc key,” aimed especially at screen-magnification users whose viewport follows the pointer. The worked example is a tooltip cleared with Escape while the pointer stays put. The hoverable clause is the standards version of the diagonal-menu problem: if the panel vanishes when the pointer leaves the trigger, the user cannot reach it, especially when magnified or using a large pointer. Exemption: nothing to dismiss if the extra content “does not obscure or replace other content.” Modals are out of scope because they should take focus, which is a change of context under 3.2.1 if it happens merely because something received focus. — [Understanding 1.4.13](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/content-on-hover-or-focus.html) and [normative 1.4.13](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#content-on-hover-or-focus)
- 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A): for every user-interface component, including links and scripted components, “the name and role can be programmatically determined; states, properties, and values that can be set by the user can be programmatically set; and notification of changes to these items is available to user agents, including assistive technologies.” A header button that opens navigation is in scope (name such as the visible “Menu” word, role, expanded/collapsed state). — [WCAG 2.2, 4.1.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#name-role-value)
- 2.5.3 Label in Name (Level A): if a control has a visible text label, “the name contains the text that is presented visually.” Note: “A best practice is to have the text of the label at the start of the name.” An icon button whose accessible name does not include the visible word fails. — [WCAG 2.2, 2.5.3](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#label-in-name)
- 3.2.1 On Focus (Level A): “When any user interface component receives focus, it does not initiate a change of context.” Opening a menu is not automatically a change of context; moving focus, loading a new page, or opening a new window on mere focus is. — [WCAG 2.2, 3.2.1](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#on-focus)
- 3.2.3 Consistent Navigation (Level AA): repeated navigation “occur[s] in the same relative order each time,” unless the user caused the change. 3.2.4 Consistent Identification (Level AA): same function, same identification. — [WCAG 2.2, 3.2.3](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#consistent-navigation)
- 2.4.4 Link Purpose in Context (Level A) and 2.4.6 Headings and Labels (Level AA) apply to header links and the skip link’s name. 2.4.5 Multiple Ways (Level AA) requires more than one way to locate a page in the set, “except where the web page is the result of, or a step in, a process.” A header nav plus search is a common pair of ways; a step inside a checkout-like flow is the exception, not an ordinary lesson. — [WCAG 2.2, 2.4.5](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#multiple-ways)
- 3.2.6 Consistent Help (Level A, new in 2.2): if human contact details, a human contact mechanism, self-help, or a fully automated contact mechanism are repeated across the set, “they occur in the same order relative to other page content.” Help may be “a direct link.” This hits a header only when the header is where help is repeated. — [WCAG 2.2, 3.2.6](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#consistent-help)
- Other header-adjacent AA criteria confirmed in the same Recommendation, not re-quoted in full: 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for large text; 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast 3:1 for control boundaries and states (the 2.4.11 understanding document says a focus indicator must still meet 1.4.11 when a translucent overlay dims it); 1.4.4 Resize Text to 200% without losing header content or function; 1.4.10 Reflow at 320 CSS pixels width; 1.4.12 Text Spacing, no loss of content when line height, paragraph, letter, and word spacing are increased, which is a known way sticky bars and single-line navs clip. 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (Level A) is what makes a header’s list of links and its current-item state available programmatically. 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (Level A) applies if header motion starts by itself, lasts more than five seconds, and runs in parallel with other content. — [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)

### Inferences

- For a sticky lesson header, 2.4.11 is the criterion that changed the conformance bar in 2.2: keyboard focus that scrolls under the bar must remain at least partly visible, which is why `scroll-padding` is the documented fix. A hide-on-scroll header that covers the focused link only while it is animating back in is the case the understanding document is written around.
- “Escape closes the menu” is required in practice when the menu is the kind of hover/focus popup 1.4.13 describes and the popup covers other content: some dismiss gesture that does not move the pointer or the keyboard focus is mandatory, and Escape is the mechanism W3C shows. A click-opened disclosure that does not appear on hover or focus is outside 1.4.13’s trigger condition, but if it traps focus it still fails 2.1.2, and if it covers the next focused control it is in 2.4.11 unless Escape, scroll, or an equivalent reveals that control without moving focus forward.
- 24 CSS pixels (AA) and 44 CSS pixels (AAA) and NN/g’s 1cm touch target are three different bars. Meeting 2.5.8 does not meet the NN/g touch guideline or 2.5.5. Inline breadcrumb links can use the inline exception under both 2.5.8 and 2.5.5; a row of header buttons cannot.

### Gaps

- The WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices menu-button and disclosure-navigation examples were not opened in this pass. Those are the usual source for “Escape returns focus to the menu button” and for the warning not to use `role="menu"` for site navigation. They are not WCAG success criteria. Do not treat them as quoted here.
- 2.4.13 Focus Appearance (AAA) was not extracted beyond its listing as a new criterion (focus-indicator size and contrast).
- No conformance-test report on education-site headers was found. Technique IDs cited (G1, ARIA11, H69, C43, F110) come from the understanding pages that were opened.

# What mobile navigation patterns have evidence, and for which jobs?

### Takeaway

The strongest website evidence is NN/g 2016, N=179, six live sites, phones and desktops: hiding primary links under a hamburger or similar control cut use of navigation, slowed tasks, raised rated difficulty, and lowered a “found it via navigation” discoverability metric, on both phone and desktop, with a larger time penalty on desktop. They still recommend hiding some links on phones when there are more than four top-level items, because the alternatives they considered were worse, and they did not test a fully visible phone nav. A visible-plus-hidden combination beat hidden-only. A 2016 master’s study (N=20) found a bottom bar faster than a hamburger for a three-level demo app. A 2024 coffee-shop app study’s completion rates were 99% versus 98%, which is not a meaningful difference. NN/g’s pattern guide assigns hamburgers to browse-mostly sites where content is the product, tab bars to about five frequent destinations, and a homepage hub to single-task tools.

### Cited Findings

- Kara Pernice and Raluca Budiu, 26 June 2016. Remote unmoderated tests with WhatUsersDo: “179 participants who completed tasks on 6 different sites, both on smartphones and desktops.” Conditions: hidden (icon or button), visible (links in a bar), and combo (some links visible, some inside a menu). “For mobile devices, we did not fully test the visible-navigation condition.” Two tasks per site; tasks did not tell people to use the menu. Content discoverability was not raw completion: people “were actually able to complete them most of the time.” Discoverability counted whether they found content “by using navigation (and not search)” when it was not linked from the homepage. — [NN/g, Hamburger Menus and Hidden Navigation Hurt UX Metrics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/) and [methodology companion](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hidden-navigation-methodology/)
- Navigation use. Desktop: hidden menus used in “27% of the cases,” visible “48%,” combo “50%.” Mobile: hidden “57%,” combo “86%” (“1.5 times more”). Differences “statistically significant” (mixed-effects logistic regression). Mobile use of navigation, hidden or not, was higher than desktop. Time to first use, only when navigation was used: desktop hidden took “5–7 seconds more”; the chart description says about 31 seconds hidden versus 23 combo and 26 visible. Mobile: about 26 seconds hidden versus 24 combo (“about 2 seconds”). The model for “took longer when hidden” was “marginally significant (p < 0.1).” A device-by-type interaction was “not statistically significant.” — [NN/g, Hamburger Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)
- Experience metrics, same study. Discoverability “showed a more than 20% drop” for hidden versus visible or combo. The article summary says “Discoverability is cut almost in half.” Those two wordings do not match. In the 17 February 2017 video of the same study, Pernice says mobile content discoverability was “64%” hidden and “85%” combo. 85 to 64 is a 21-point drop, consistent with “more than 20%,” not with “almost in half.” Desktop discoverability percentages were not in the transcript excerpt. — [Article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/) and [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rdeoPuPzONg)
- Task difficulty, 1 = easy and 7 = difficult, “aggregated data over both mobile and desktop”: chart description hidden 2.6, combo 2.3, visible 2.1. Authors report a “21% increase” hidden versus visible and “11%” versus combo. Task time: desktop “at least 39% slower” when navigation was hidden; mobile “15% slower” than combo. Across navigation types, “mobile users took 12% more time.” Hidden navigation “is more likely to reduce content discoverability on desktop than on mobile.” — [NN/g, Hamburger Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)
- Why, in their words: low salience of a small icon, “low information scent” (the icon does not say what is inside), extra step to open, inconsistent placement, and, in 2016, low familiarity especially on desktop. They write that familiarity “might decline in future years” as a problem, but “low salience, low information scent, and extra work will always be a curse on hidden navigation.” One desktop example from the same study, Slate’s hamburger in the upper right: “Only 17% of people used it, as opposed to the 42% total average of desktop navigation used,” and “33 seconds to find, compared to the desktop average of 25 seconds.” — [NN/g, Hamburger Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/) and [desktop discoverability companion, 24 July 2016](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/find-navigation-desktop-not-hamburger/)
- Their design conclusion, which is a recommendation on top of those numbers: do not hide desktop primary navigation; show top-level links across the top or down the left. On phones, show the links if there are “4 or fewer”; if more than 4, “the usability penalty imposed by hidden navigation is very present on mobile, but it is smaller than on the desktop,” so they accept hiding some links and ask for in-page links to important items. A word label such as Menu beat an unlabeled icon in “third-party research” they cite but this pass did not open (`exisweb.net/menu-eats-hamburger`): NN/g’s phrase is “slightly more popular with users.” — [NN/g, Hamburger Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/) and [mobile patterns, 15 November 2015](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-navigation-patterns/)
- Pattern-to-job mapping, Budiu, 15 November 2015, from qualitative mobile testing, before the 2016 numbers were published. Top bars and tab bars “work well only when there are relatively few navigation options”; “more than 5 options” is hard “and still keep an optimum touch-target size.” A carousel of tabs fails when hidden categories are not predictable (“out of sight is out of mind”). Tab bars are persistent; many top bars scroll away unless made sticky. The hamburger “can contain a fairly large number of navigation options in a tiny space” and “makes the navigation options least discoverable.” It “is best suited for content-heavy, browse-mostly sites and apps” such as news, where people mostly read what is in front of them and the menu “steal[s] a minimum amount of space from content.” A homepage hub fits “task-based” tools when the user does one branch per session (the United Airlines check-in example) and is a poor fit when people switch sections often, because every switch goes back through the hub. Gesture-only menus with no button “most users would never discover.” — [NN/g, Basic Patterns for Mobile Navigation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-navigation-patterns/)
- Bottom bar versus hamburger, master’s thesis, KTH, 2016, N=20, custom demo app, three-level navigation. Record abstract: bottom bar “is more efficient than hamburger menu in terms of total completion time,” and participants “perceive bottom bar menu better.” Full-text excerpt: mean total time 28.26 s bottom bar versus 36.39 s hamburger, “22.3% less,” “p = 0.006.” Per-task, only one task was significant on its own (“32.8% faster, p = 0.001”). Clicks averaged 8.7 versus 12.2; the excerpt says the click difference was not significant and prints “p = 4.32,” which is not a possible p-value, so the click statistic should not be used. Preference: “12 of them (60%)” chose the bottom bar. Not a website, not a reading task, student sample, one prototype. — [KTH record](https://kth.diva-portal.org/smash/record.jsf?pid=diva2:922114) and [full text PDF](http://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:922114/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- Bottom bar versus hamburger, coffee-shop ordering app, 2024 9th International Conference on Business and Industrial Research, 23–24 May 2024. Abstract only: SUS plus task scenarios. “99 percent task completion” for the bottom bar and “98 percent” for the hamburger. The abstract still concludes the bottom bar is “more effective.” Sample size, SUS scores, and time are not in the abstract retrieved. A one-point completion difference at a ceiling is not evidence of a practical gap. — [IEEE Xplore, document 10875908](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10875908/)
- Baymard mobile homepage and category benchmark, collection page updated 30 September 2025: “the performance for up to 67% of leading US and European sites is ‘mediocre’ to ‘poor’.” This is an audit score across their guideline set, not a hamburger-versus-tab experiment. Mobile menus in their benchmark are “by default collapsed and hidden behind a ‘3-bars’ hamburger icon,” and the failure they describe is reaching both the parent category and deep subcategories (see the 24% “View All” finding above). — [Baymard, Homepage and category articles](https://baymard.com/blog/collections/homepage-and-category) and [mobile navigation examples](https://baymard.com/mcommerce-usability/benchmark/mobile-page-types/navigation-menu)
- A 2026 merchant-blog table claiming tab bars raise pages per session by “33–52%” and conversion by “12–28%” cites “Navi+ merchant aggregate data and Google/Baymard” without a study link. It is not usable as evidence. — not cited as a finding.

### Inferences

- The 2016 study’s jobs were lightweight content finding on news and similar sites (“find a technology article that interests you,” BBC wildlife). That is closer to browsing a library than to checking out, and it is not lesson comprehension. The measured cost of hiding links showed up even on those loose tasks.
- NN/g’s own split is: desktop, do not hide; phone, hide only the overflow past about four links, and do not hide the links people need in order to do the main job. Their 2015 job split puts a hamburger on sites where reading the current page is the visit, and a persistent tab bar on sites where people switch among a few sections. A technical lesson is the first job while the reader is in the article, and a track library is the second job when they came to choose a track. The research does not test a page that is both.
- Bottom bars have one small significant time result (N=20, app prototype, 2016) and one null-looking completion result (2024 abstract). They are not supported at the same evidence level as the N=179 hidden-versus-visible website study. The 2016 NN/g study did not include a bottom bar, so it cannot be cited as evidence against one.
- Using the 2016 familiarity caveat in 2026: the authors expected recognition of the icon to improve and expected the extra tap and the missing label scent not to go away. No later NN/g replication with new percentages was found.

### Gaps

- No replication of the N=179 study after hamburger icons became common. The methodology page was not fully read; the six site names and the per-site task list are only partly visible in the public articles.
- Visible-only navigation was not tested on phones in that study, so “visible beats hidden on mobile” is not a direct contrast. Combo versus hidden is the mobile contrast they have.
- No study retrieved compares hamburger, top links, and bottom bar on a long-form reading site, a documentation site, or a course player.
- The coffee-shop paper’s SUS scores and N were not available from the abstract. The KTH click p-value as printed is not interpretable.
- Material Design’s “3 to 5 destinations” bottom-navigation rule was not re-fetched; it is a platform guideline, not a study, and is not quoted here.
