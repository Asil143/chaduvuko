# Keep the site bar while reading

A world-class header for a public written-education library in 2026 is a short wayfinding bar that stays in place when a lesson opens, not a study bar that deletes the site. On 9 October 2026 the written courses whose chrome actually rendered — javascript.info, MDN Learn, web.dev Learn, React’s learn pages, the Next.js getting-started guide, The Odin Project, the Rust book, and fast.ai — kept product navigation and search in the top bar, and put the lesson title, the track outline, and previous/next somewhere else. The only fetched page that replaced a marketing header with a study bar was a DeepLearning.AI video lesson behind a login wall, a player rather than a reading library. Chaduvuko should use one bar on the catalog and on the lesson: a home mark, **Lessons, Roadmaps, Practice, and Interview**, a visible search whose index includes those destinations, and a single **Sign in**. It should refuse a resume button, a progress meter, an AI control, the eighteen tracks, and previous/next inside that bar. Those are the pieces the written sites leave out, and they are the pieces a 320px row cannot hold without clipping.

## Written courses leave the site bar in place

The common “study bar” — lesson title, a track menu, previous and next, and a thin account control, with the rest of the site removed — is not what the best written-course pages do. javascript.info keeps its toolbar on the Variables article (search, theme, language, the ebook link, the tutorial map) and places **“Previous lesson” and “Next lesson” at the article boundary**, not in that toolbar ([javascript.info](https://javascript.info/variables)). MDN’s global header on a Learn lesson is still the product header: search, “Toggle navigation,” and the tabs HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Web APIs, All, Learn, Tools, and About. The lesson title is the `h1`. Previous and next sit in `ul.prev-next`, and “In this article” is a sidebar ([Basic HTML syntax](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development/Core/Structuring_content/Basic_HTML_syntax)). web.dev’s chapter “Document structure” keeps the same site header as the rest of the site and adds “Hide side navigation,” “On this page,” and, at the end of the article, Previous and Next naming the neighbor chapters ([Document structure](https://web.dev/learn/html/document-structure)). React’s learn page uses one sticky product nav — React, a version link, Search with ⌘ / Ctrl K, Learn, Reference, Community, Blog, theme — while the course outline is a sidebar and the page ends with a Next link to the following lesson ([Describing the UI](https://react.dev/learn/describing-the-ui)). The same top labels appear on the `useState` reference page ([useState](https://react.dev/reference/react/useState)). Next.js, Vercel’s docs, Svelte, and the Rust book follow that split: product bar, sidebar for the ordered list, neighbor links after the article or, in the Rust book only, previous/next chapter arrows outside the article text ([Layouts and Pages](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/getting-started/layouts-and-pages), [Getting started with Vercel](https://vercel.com/docs/getting-started-with-vercel), [`$state`](https://svelte.dev/docs/svelte/$state), [Variables and Mutability](https://doc.rust-lang.org/book/ch03-01-variables-and-mutability.html)).

The Odin Project is the closest “many lessons, account optional” check that opened. Its lesson page keeps **All Paths, About, Community, Support us, Sign in, and Get Started**. The `h1` “How Does the Web Work?” is a content header. “Lesson contents” is an aside hidden below the `xl` breakpoint. At the bottom of the lesson the controls are Previous, the text “Sign in to track progress,” and Next ([How Does the Web Work?](https://www.theodinproject.com/lessons/foundations-how-does-the-web-work)). fast.ai keeps the course name in a fixed header and the lesson list in a sidebar; previous/next are page navigation, and “On this page” includes the video ([Lesson 1](https://course.fast.ai/Lessons/lesson1.html)). None of these pages put the lesson title in the product header as the main heading.

The contrasting page is real, and it is the wrong class of product. On a DeepLearning.AI lesson URL the marketing links (Courses, News, Blog, Membership, For Business) are gone. The lesson header is a module trail, a sidebar toggle, Previous lesson, Next lesson, Syllabus, Courses, and Log in, and the page tells the visitor to log in to continue ([AI Python for Beginners](https://learn.deeplearning.ai/courses/ai-python-for-beginners/lesson/z57gn/introduction)). Logged-out fetches never reached the players for Khan Academy exercises, Duolingo lessons, freeCodeCamp steps, Codecademy, Coursera lectures, or Boot.dev exercises, so those products cannot be cited as reading-header evidence. Coursera’s machine-learning URL and Master.dev’s course URL stay on the marketing header because they are sales pages, not open lessons ([Machine Learning](https://www.coursera.org/learn/machine-learning), [Next.js Fundamentals](https://master.dev/courses/next-js-v4/)).

Hiding the bar on the way down is also not an established reading pattern. fast.ai is the only page in the set that loads Headroom, and the pin and unpin rules are not in the lesson HTML, so the scroll direction was not verified ([Lesson 1](https://course.fast.ai/Lessons/lesson1.html)). React, Next.js, Tailwind, Vercel, the Rust book, and GitHub Docs pin a short bar with a sticky or fixed class ([Describing the UI](https://react.dev/learn/describing-the-ui), [flex](https://tailwindcss.com/docs/flex), [Hello World](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/using-github/hello-world)). Vercel’s bar changes background and shadow after scroll; it does not leave ([Getting started with Vercel](https://vercel.com/docs/getting-started-with-vercel)). MDN’s and web.dev’s header tags carry no sticky class, and stylesheets were not checked, so their scroll behavior is unknown. GitHub’s Primer design system says the opposite of the docs banner in one sentence: **“App header is never fixed to the top of the viewport. It scrolls with the rest of the page.”** ([Primer layout](https://primer.style/product/getting-started/foundations/layout/)). NN/g’s 2021 synthesis treats hide-on-scroll as a mobile compromise that “works moderately well” and then misfires, because any scroll upward is treated as a request for the header, including when someone scrolls up only to reread ([Sticky Headers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)). Rereading is the lesson. A second sticky strip is the failure they illustrate: on an iPhone 11 Pro, a large sticky header plus another sticky bar left about a **2:1 content-to-chrome ratio**, against about **13:1** for a small header on The New Yorker. Those ratios are single-site illustrations, not norms. No task-time study of sticky versus static versus hide-on-scroll headers was found, on lessons or on anything else.

## The track sits beside the lesson, not inside the bar

Chaduvuko’s eighteen tracks are a catalog. The sources that rewrote navigation in 2024–2026 keep a catalog out of the task header. GOV.UK’s rule is the cleanest: **“Navigation is not a site map and does not need to list every part of your service,”** links go to the most important top-level sections, and once someone is inside a service the topic menu stays off so they can finish the task ([Navigate a service](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/navigate-a-service/)). In February 2026, GOV.UK Frontend 6.0.0 removed service name and service links from the header component itself; the header is the logo and GOV.UK-wide tools, and service links live in a separate service navigation ([GOV.UK header](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/header/)). Red Hat’s primary-navigation guidelines say not to overload that bar with menus ([Red Hat guidelines](https://github.com/RedHat-UX/red-hat-design-system/blob/main/elements/rh-navigation-primary/docs/20-guidelines.md)). Primer splits the same way: the app header is global navigation, a short context line, and local navigation; the left pane is where lists and filters live; the page title is a page-header region, not the app header; and the context line **“is not a full-path breadcrumb”** ([Primer layout](https://primer.style/product/getting-started/foundations/layout/)). GOV.UK’ step-by-step pattern, the closest public note to “put the whole journey in the header,” records the failure: **users rarely use the step-by-step navigation header**, which suggests its purpose is unclear ([Step by step navigation](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/step-by-step-navigation/)).

There is no supported cap of seven items. NN/g’s point, from a 2019 explanation of Miller’s paper, is that people do not memorize a visible menu, so a bar of ten specific labels can beat three vague ones ([How Many Items in a Navigation Menu?](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/number-items-navigation-menu/)). The number that does bind a phone is smaller. After a 2016 study, NN/g’s recommendation is: **if there are four or fewer top-level links, show them; if there are more than four, hide some** ([Hamburger Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)). A 2015 pattern note says top bars and tab bars get hard above about five options if touch targets stay usable ([Basic Patterns for Mobile Navigation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-navigation-patterns/)). Chaduvuko’s real top level is four destinations — the lesson library, roadmaps, practice, and interview prep — which is inside that band. The eighteen track names are a second tier of a size NN/g treats as a catalog problem, not a bar problem. Mega menus can beat scrolling dropdowns in qualitative testing when every choice is visible at once and groups scan at a glance, and they fail when they scroll, depend on a fast hover, hide search, or shrink targets ([Mega Menus Work Well](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mega-menus-work-well/)). Nothing retrieved measured a mega menu on a lesson library. A 2024 NN/g checklist prefers **click-opened** submenus over hover, and says a simple dropdown becomes a poor tool past one tier ([Menu-Design Checklist](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/menu-design/)). WCAG 2.2’s 1.4.13, in the 12 December 2024 Recommendation, is the test that replaces the old half-second hover recipe: content that appears on hover or focus has to be dismissible, hoverable, and persistent ([Understanding 1.4.13](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/content-on-hover-or-focus.html)).

On the lesson, the track is a sidebar that can get out of the way. MDN requires a sidebar on every page and points readers at it rather than at a global map ([Sidebars](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/MDN/Writing_guidelines/Page_structures/Sidebars)). GitLab’s navigation sidebar, updated 15 July 2026, stays contextual, can be hidden, remembers that preference, and is **hidden by default below 1200px** until opened as an overlay ([Navigation sidebar](https://design.gitlab.com/patterns/navigation-sidebar)). Primer’s narrow range is under 768px and is one column: **do not stack a long link pane above the article**, because the reader has to scroll before they can read; split list and detail, or open the pane as a sheet ([Primer layout](https://primer.style/product/getting-started/foundations/layout/)). “On this page” is never a product-header control in this set. React and Next.js hide it until a `2xl` breakpoint, Vercel until `lg`, Odin until `xl` ([Describing the UI](https://react.dev/learn/describing-the-ui), [Layouts and Pages](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/getting-started/layouts-and-pages)). Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines say the opposite about a different layer: do not hide the tab bar that switches top-level areas, or people forget where they are ([Tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars)). The two rules fit together. The four site sections stay reachable. The track outline is allowed to collapse. Vercel’s February 2026 dashboard did the same kind of split for an app, not a lesson: a hideable sidebar, and a bottom bar for one-handed section switching ([Dashboard navigation redesign](https://vercel.com/changelog/dashboard-navigation-redesign-rollout)). That bottom bar was not observed as previous/next on any written lesson here. The Rust book’s mobile chapter arrows are page navigation for a single book, not a site menu ([Variables and Mutability](https://doc.rust-lang.org/book/ch03-01-variables-and-mutability.html)). A bottom lesson pager is an unobserved transplant.

Location follows the same cut. The product name stays in the bar. The section is a breadcrumb and the highlighted sidebar row. The title is the `h1`. W3C’s breadcrumb pattern puts the trail in its own navigation landmark, horizontally before the main content, with the current page marked current ([Breadcrumb pattern](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/breadcrumb/)). GOV.UK places breadcrumbs just before `<main`, so “Skip to main content” skips them with the navigation ([Navigate a service](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/navigate-a-service/)). NN/g, in guidance last reviewed 1 September 2026, still wants a hierarchy trail rather than a session history, says the trail does not replace global or local navigation, marks the current page as text rather than a link, and says a purely linear site gains little from breadcrumbs ([Breadcrumbs](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/breadcrumbs/)). Chaduvuko is not that linear minisite. A lesson sits in a track, the track sits in a library, and people arrive from search and from shared links, which is the case NN/g says the trail is for. On a phone, the same article says a wrapped trail pushes the title down and that the practical cut is **the parent only**. Two observed styles both keep the title out of the product bar: MDN includes the current page as the last crumb and still uses an `h1`; Stripe, Kubernetes, and GitHub Docs stop the linked crumbs at the parent ([How Checkout works](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/checkout/how-checkout-works), [Pods](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/pods/), [Hello World](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/using-github/hello-world)).

## Search has to be seen and has to cover the bar

The written libraries that rendered put search in the header: MDN (“Search the site”), javascript.info (“Search on Javascript.info” and, in the tablet menu, “Search in the tutorial”), web.dev, freeCodeCamp (“Search 13,000+ of our books and courses”), and roadmap.sh (⌘ K) ([Basic HTML syntax](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development/Core/Structuring_content/Basic_HTML_syntax), [freeCodeCamp curriculum](https://www.freecodecamp.org/learn), [roadmap.sh frontend](https://roadmap.sh/frontend)). Odin’s lesson has no search field. Developer docs often reduce the control to a button that names a shortcut — React, Next.js, Tailwind, Stripe’s “Search /”, GitHub’s “Search or ask Copilot” — and keep the results out of the bar ([Describing the UI](https://react.dev/learn/describing-the-ui), [How Checkout works](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/checkout/how-checkout-works)).

For a public library, the older NN/g finding still points at a field people can see. Users look for a type-in box, conventionally at the upper right, and on one homepage in 2001 replacing a search link with a box coincided with a **91% increase in search use**, which is a single-site before/after, not a multi-site experiment ([Search: Visible and Simple](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/search-visible-and-simple/)). Later write-ups repeat the desktop rule: an open field, not an icon that must be expanded, and not a field hidden inside a menu. On a small screen the same sources allow the field to wait behind a magnifying-glass icon, and they name the cost as extra taps ([The Magnifying-Glass Icon in Search Design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/magnifying-glass-icon/), [Intranet-Search Essentials](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/intranet-search/)). No study in this pass compared a command palette with a visible field on a documentation or course site. The nearest 2026 experiment is about mobile command selection, not finding a lesson: with 120 participants, a one-layer toolbar of visible commands was **347–459ms faster (27–36%)** than keyboard methods, and a toolbar that required an extra navigation step was slower than the shortcuts ([GI ’26 PDF](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~dvogel/gi2026/papers/1026b.pdf)). GitHub’s own palette is a power-user tool. On 16 July 2025 GitHub paused a plan to remove it because qualitative feedback said it was integral even where metrics undercounted it ([Pausing Command Palette deprecation](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-07-15-upcoming-deprecation-of-github-command-palette-feature-preview/)). That is a reason to let Command-K focus a search field. It is not a reason to make a hidden palette the only search on a site where accounts are optional and most readers are not in a keyboard product all day. A single-character shortcut would also have to satisfy WCAG 2.1.4; a shortcut that already includes Ctrl or Command does not fall under that “letter only” condition ([WCAG 2.2, 2.1.4](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#character-key-shortcuts)).

Search and the bar are supposed to be two indexes of one service. GOV.UK puts in-service search in service navigation and says the placeholder should name that service ([Navigate a service](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/navigate-a-service/)). In NN/g’s 2016 navigation study, hidden menus cut a “found it via navigation, not search” discoverability measure by **more than 20 percent** against visible or combination navigation, and desktop users fell back to search more readily ([Hamburger Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)). The companion video’s mobile figures, **64% hidden versus 85% combination**, match a drop of about 21 points; the article’s phrase “almost in half” does not, so the 21-point figure is the one to keep ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rdeoPuPzONg)). Search is the fallback when the menu has weak scent. It does not replace the menu, and the menu does not replace it ([Basic Patterns for Mobile Navigation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-navigation-patterns/)). Chaduvuko’s search indexes lessons and tracks, while roadmaps and interview pages are real destinations it can miss. That gap was not found as a published bug on another site. It is still the failure GOV.UK’s and GitHub’s rules are written against: the header offers a place the search cannot find. The control in the bar has to search the library, the roadmaps, the practice pages, and the interview pages, and the placeholder has to say so. What each observed search engine actually indexes was not stated beyond the labels on the controls; Algolia, Pagefind, cmdk, and Lunr names identify an implementation, not a corpus.

“Ask AI” is current furniture on flagship docs and was not found retracted. Next.js shows “Search… ⌘K” and “Ask AI” together, Stripe exposes an Ask AI header control, Vercel puts “Ask AI” in the marketing header, GitHub combines the jobs into “Search or ask Copilot,” and Coursera’s marketing bar has “Ask Coursera” ([Layouts and Pages](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/getting-started/layouts-and-pages), [How Checkout works](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/checkout/how-checkout-works), [Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/)). No primary post in this pass calls the button harmful. The written public libraries did not ship it. javascript.info, MDN, web.dev Learn, Odin, and freeCodeCamp have no AI control in the bar. roadmap.sh exposes “AI Tutor” as a section link and as a switch on the roadmap page, which is a product area, not a second action beside search ([roadmap.sh frontend](https://roadmap.sh/frontend)). On a 320px reading bar the cost of copying Next.js is a target, not a trend to join.

## A 320px row is why the extra controls go

The phone constraint is normative even though no education header was measured at 320px in this pass. WCAG 2.2’s 1.4.10 requires reflow at **320 CSS pixels** without loss of content or function, and 1.4.12 says text-spacing increases must not clip the bar ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)). Primer names an `xsmall` breakpoint at **320px**, sets horizontal padding there to **16px** (about a 288px column before a scrollbar), and says pages adapt to smaller screens without losing function ([Primer layout](https://primer.style/product/getting-started/foundations/layout/)). Pointer targets at AA are **at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels** (2.5.8); AAA asks for **44 by 44** (2.5.5); NN/g’s touch guidance is about **1cm by 1cm** ([Understanding 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html), [Sticky Headers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)). Meeting 24px does not meet the other two. A row that tries to hold a logo, a menu button, search, a theme icon, and an account icon inside that 288px column is the overflow the layout rules describe. It was inferred from the controls observed on docs sites, not photographed.

The 2016 NN/g study is still the only large comparison of hidden versus visible website navigation found here: **179 participants, six live sites, phones and desktops**. Hidden navigation was used on desktop in **27%** of cases against **48%** visible and **50%** combination, and on phones in **57%** of cases against **86%** for the combination. Desktop task time was **at least 39% slower** when navigation was hidden; mobile was **15% slower** than the combination. The study did **not** fully test a visible-only phone nav, so “show every link on a phone” is not a result they measured ([Hamburger Menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/), [methodology](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hidden-navigation-methodology/)). Their design conclusion, which is a recommendation on top of those numbers, is the one that survives contact with 320px: do not hide primary navigation on desktop; on a phone, hide only the overflow, and label the button. A word such as Menu beat an unlabeled icon in third-party research they cite and this pass did not re-open. The authors expected the hamburger to become more familiar and expected the extra tap and the missing label to remain costly. No later replication with new percentages was found. The small-screen pattern the reading pages actually encode, via classes rather than a resized browser, is the same minimum: a product mark, a menu button, and search. Sidebars and “On this page” become panels ([Describing the UI](https://react.dev/learn/describing-the-ui), [Hello World](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/using-github/hello-world)).

That width budget is also why several “header features” of 2023–2026 stay off Chaduvuko’s bar even though serious sites have not deleted them as a class. Announcement strips are not gone: roadmap.sh still opens a roadmap with a partner line, Exercism pins a Jiki announcement, and an edX page in this pass carried a promo banner ([roadmap.sh frontend](https://roadmap.sh/frontend), [Exercism](https://exercism.org/)). NN/g’s 2018 eyetracking write-up, with no newer replacement found, says people ignore the top of the page when it resembles an ad, including legitimate content placed there ([Banner Blindness Revisited](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/)). A marketing strip plus a sticky bar is the stacked chrome that produces the 2:1 phone. Social icons were in Svelte’s docs nav and in CS50’s course header, and in the footer on roadmap.sh ([`$state`](https://svelte.dev/docs/svelte/$state), [roadmap.sh frontend](https://roadmap.sh/frontend)). Primer’s footer is where useful links and legal lines go, below the fold, so the page can stay on the content ([Primer layout](https://primer.style/product/getting-started/foundations/layout/)). Theme controls are live on javascript.info, React, Next.js, and Odin, and freeCodeCamp puts Night Mode inside the menu rather than in the row ([freeCodeCamp](https://www.freecodecamp.org/)). GitHub documents theme switching as a command-palette action, so it does not need a header icon ([GitHub Command Palette](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/accessibility/github-command-palette)). Language switchers are specified when the whole service exists in more than one language ([Navigate a service](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/navigate-a-service/)). Chaduvuko is not that service. A tagline on roadmap.sh sits under the page title, and Primer treats a summary as part of the page header, not the app bar.

Progress and resume are the loudest absence. No logged-out catalog header in this pass showed a progress meter, a streak, or a continue control. freeCodeCamp says “Sign in to save your progress” in the curriculum body. roadmap.sh’s “Join 146,905+ others…” and “Sign up free” are body copy. Odin’s progress line sits between Previous and Next. javascript.info’s only measured indicator is **“Lesson 8 of 94,”** a lesson index on a `progress` element, not a scroll percentage ([Variables](https://javascript.info/variables)). web.dev’s `devsite-progress` element is empty in the fetched HTML and is not evidence of a reading line. No study found here tests a header Resume against return rate or completion. Wait-state research is about a system that is busy: in the Nah experiment as NN/g summarizes it, people shown a moving indicator were willing to wait about **three times longer** than people shown nothing ([Progress Indicators](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)). That is not course completion. W3C’s cognitive-accessibility pattern separates a breadcrumb from a step list and is guidance, not a user test ([Make Each Step Clear](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o1p04-clear-steps/)). The transferable status-tracker finding is negative: if people are not shown that a tracker exists, they do not use it ([Status Trackers](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/status-trackers/)). Khan Academy’s June 2026 reimagined-product note marks site navigation and search as improved and records a student learning queue; it does not describe a header resume button ([Reimagined experience](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/46056304557837-What-do-administrators-need-to-know-about-the-Khan-Academy-reimagined-experience)). Duolingo’s published “back to where I was” control is a floating arrow at the bottom right of a long path, from a May 2022 article that was not rechecked inside a 2026 lesson, and it is not in the header ([New Duolingo home screen](https://blog.duolingo.com/new-duolingo-home-screen-design/)). NN/g’s sticky-header test asks whether people will log in from any page in a session ([Sticky Headers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)). Chaduvuko stores progress in the browser and treats the account as optional, so a join button beside Sign in fails that test. Odin still shows Sign in and Get Started; freeCodeCamp shows Sign in alone. The single account action is the one that matches an optional account. Login was not observed to have been removed from education libraries in any 2023–2026 changelog found here.

A sticky bar that covers the next focused link fails WCAG 2.2’s **2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum)**, level AA, in the 12 December 2024 Recommendation. Partial cover passes AA; full cover fails. Sticky headers are a named cause. The repair the understanding document and the implementation notes agree on is `scroll-padding` (or `scroll-margin`) equal to the bar, not a second copy of the link ([Understanding 2.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-not-obscured-minimum.html), [TPGi](https://www.tpgi.com/prevent-focused-elements-from-being-obscured-by-sticky-headers/)). The phone menu has to be a disclosure, not a fake modal: W3C’s disclosure pattern toggles on Enter or Space and lets Tab leave; a dialog traps Tab, closes on Escape, moves focus in and back, and may set `aria-modal="true"` only when the page behind is actually inert ([Disclosure pattern](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/disclosure/), [Dialog pattern](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/dialog-modal/)). A skip link is the bypass for a bar that repeats on every page (2.4.1). The visible label of a control has to be in its accessible name (2.5.3). Repeated navigation stays in the same order (3.2.3) ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)).

## Conclusion

Chaduvuko’s header is one short, opaque, sticky row on the catalog and on the lesson, with `scroll-padding` equal to its height, and it does not change between those pages. On a wide screen the row is, in order: a skip link, the home mark, the text links **Lessons, Roadmaps, Practice, and Interview**, an open search field at the right whose placeholder names the whole library, and one **Sign in**. Practice is a real link to the practice section; a click-opened disclosure under it lists the code playground, the SQL playground, and projects, and it does not open on hover. The Menu on a 320px screen is a disclosure that does not trap Tab. The phone row keeps only the home mark, a search button that opens the same field, and a button whose visible name is **Menu**. Lessons, Roadmaps, Practice and its three tools, Interview, and Sign in live in that menu. Desktop does not hide the four sections, because that is where the 2016 study measured the larger penalty. The phone hides them one tap inside a named Menu, because four text labels plus search plus an account control do not reflow inside Primer’s 320px column, and because the study’s phone result favors a visible-plus-hidden combination over an unlabeled icon. Search covers lessons, tracks, roadmaps, practice, and interview pages. Command-K may only focus that field.

On a lesson the bar is the same bar. The page, not the bar, adds a short breadcrumb immediately before the article (on a phone, the track only; the current page is not a link), the lesson title as the `h1`, a track outline that collapses and must not stack above the text on a narrow screen, an “on this page” list only when the window is wide, and Previous and Next at the end of the article, each naming the neighbor. The header refuses, on both pages: the eighteen tracks, the lesson title, previous and next, a scroll-progress line, a completion percent, a streak, a Resume or Continue control, Ask AI, a command palette in place of the field, a second row, hide-on-scroll, an announcement strip, social icons, a tagline, a language switcher, a pricing or donate control, a theme icon, a join button beside Sign in, and a bottom lesson pager. Theme, if it exists, goes inside the Menu the way freeCodeCamp’s Night Mode does, not in the row. Returning readers do need a way back to the lesson the browser already remembers, and no written-course header in this evidence provides one. Putting Resume in the bar would copy a control none of these sites ship and none of these studies measured. The tools they do ship for “where is this lesson” are the sidebar, the breadcrumb, and the named next page. The study bar that deletes the site nav remains a video-player pattern, observed once, behind a login wall, and it is the wrong header for this library.
